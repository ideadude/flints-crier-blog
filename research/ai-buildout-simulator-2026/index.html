The Verdict

Partly an overreaction — just not in the names everyone is staring at.

The chip selloff is roughly fair. Starting from what the market paid on Nov 3, Nvidia, Micron and SK Hynix at today's prices imply that ~24–28% of the US data center pipeline gets blocked. That is almost exactly Brad Gerstner's 25–35% estimate. If you believe Gerstner, MU at ~$688 is fairly priced for the politics — it's not a gift.

The overreaction is in the second-order names. Power gear (Vertiv, Eaton, GE Vernova) and Dell are priced as if more than 70% of the pipeline vanishes, and TSMC, Sandisk, Arm and the equipment makers as if 45–60% does. Their demand depends less on any one county commission. Sold-out turbines, optics and NAND don't un-sell because Ohio passed 21 moratoria.

The underreaction is in leverage. CoreWeave and Oracle imply only 12–18% blocked, and so do Intel and AMD. They're the ones holding contracted chips with nowhere to plug them in. If the revolt is real, the balance sheet is where it shows up first.

The bigger risk for memory isn't politics, it's 2028. Every maker says it's sold out through 2027, and SK Hynix says through 2029. But Micron Idaho, Hynix Yongin, Samsung P5 and CXMT all land in 2027–28. Click the "Memory glut 2027" scenario and MU loses more than half its value. The election moved the date by a few months, at most.

How to read this

Bars near 25–35%: priced about where the best-informed estimates sit. NVDA, MU, SK Hynix and ASML are here. The selloff in the headline names is not crazy. It's the market doing math.

Bars at 40–70%+: the stock fell more than its exposure to US permits justifies, if permits were the only thing that changed. That covers TSMC, Broadcom, Marvell, Sandisk, Arm, the equipment makers, the power-gear names, Dell and Rambus. These are the overreaction candidates. Before you trust it, ask what else hit them: rates, a Samsung guide, crowded positioning.

Bars under 20%: the price still assumes the politics mostly fizzle. That covers CoreWeave, Oracle, Intel and AMD. Arista sits here too, but only because it barely fell. If you think Gerstner is right, the levered ones have further to fall.

Not shown (no answer in range): hyperscalers. MSFT, GOOGL and AMZN rose after the election. In the model, a blocked buildout makes their existing compute more valuable (scarcity rent) and their capex bill smaller, which is Clifton's point exactly. CEG, VST and META also show no answer: their drops come from price-cap politics or other drivers, not permit risk.

Where this came from

This started in an InvestorGeeks thread. Jason dropped TCAf 267, Dan Clifton (Strategas head of policy research) on The Compound after the midterms, whose argument is that the data center trade is the entire market story and the election just broke it. Then came the forward P/E screenshot that started the memory conversation (SNDK 2.9×, SK Hynix 3.2×, MU 3.2×), a stock-research run on MU (accumulate in tranches at $650 / $531 / $470), and a mainstreet build (probability-weighted value ~$495, real margin-of-safety buys under ~$330). The question underneath all of it: is the post-election dump in chips and AI infrastructure an overreaction, and how would you even tell? This report is the "how would you tell" part.

Corrections to numbers in the thread. It was 40 of 41 local measures that went against the industry (Ballotpedia, WSJ), not 38 of 40. Texas's Dec 17 date is a PUCT open meeting, not a legislative hearing. ERCOT's audit report is due Dec 10. The Blackstone/QTS Prince William Digital Gateway died in court in July, and Amazon's Ashburn withdrawal was a local-opposition deal. Neither was caused by Virginia's new data center electricity tax, which is $0.011/kWh, capped at $600M a year and sunsetting in 2028. I could not find a sourced link between the debt ceiling and repealing 100% expensing. Expensing is permanent under OBBBA. The live threat is Wyden's draft excise tax, which is unlikely to pass in a lame duck.

What actually happened Nov 3

  • Ballots: 41 local data center measures in 10 states; 40 went against the industry (27 limits passed, 13 pro-development measures defeated). Ohio had 21, all anti. Of the 47 decided in 2026, 46 favored limits. Exit polls: 56% called AI/data centers a major factor and 76% oppose a data center in their own area (Ballotpedia, CNBC).
  • Governors-elect in Texas (Hinojosa), Ohio (Acton, "conditional moratorium"), Georgia and Michigan ran on pauses or stricter rules. About 40% of planned US capacity sits in states that want to hit pause (Cleanview via Bloomberg).
  • Texas was the grail. Abbott ordered an ERCOT/PUCT audit of the large-load queue in August: ~474 GW requested, ~90% data centers, against a ~91 GW record peak. "Batch Zero" (≥75 MW) is paused and TCEQ was told to halt data center permits. ERCOT's report is due Dec 10 and the PUCT meets Dec 17. Hinojosa appoints the PUCT and promises a moratorium that outlasts the audit (Express-News, Lloyd Gosselink).
  • Ohio has ~138–147 active local moratoria. Trenton's ban hits a ~1 GW Amazon campus. AEP Ohio now makes >25 MW loads pay up to 85% minimum (Ohio Capital Journal).
  • OpenAI leaders discussed whether to reset 2027 capacity expectations. That was a discussion, not a number (WSJ).

Federal and global levers

  • Tariffs: SCOTUS struck down the IEEPA tariffs 6–3 on Feb 20, and ~$166–175B of refunds are flowing (Wiley). The Section 232 semiconductor tariff is 25% on a narrow set of advanced chips, with broad exemptions for US data centers. The slider asks what happens if those exemptions go.
  • China: ~600,000 H200s are sitting in Hong Kong because Beijing won't approve imports (Reuters). Nvidia guides ~zero China data center revenue. Washington is no longer the binding constraint on China sales. Beijing is. Equipment makers carry far more China exposure than chip designers now, which is why ASML, LRCX and AMAT move most on the export slider.
  • Debt ceiling: limit $41.1T, X-date ~mid-2027, funding expires ~Dec 11 (CNBC). It's a leverage point, but the "kill 100% expensing" route is unsourced. The slider defaults to 20% odds.
  • Can it relocate? Some of it. Stargate UAE is a 5 GW campus with the first 200 MW targeted for late 2026. The Middle East has ~1.2 GW live and is heading toward 3+ GW in 2027. The Nordics have ~2.1 GW, India ~1.6–1.8 GW (Epoch). That's single-digit GW against 40–50 GW a year of planned US adds, and Gulf builds are gated by export licenses. Most relocation is within the US, to friendlier states, and it costs a year. The 45% default is generous for 2027 and plausible for 2028+.

Nov 5 was the big one. The SOX fell 12.3%, its worst day since March 2020, and the Nasdaq fell 5.5% into a correction. Hyperscalers rose. Note the memory slide started before the election: SNDK was already −36% in the six sessions before Nov 5, and MU dove on Oct 30 after Samsung's guide (MarketWatch, TIKR).

Clifton's best point, tested

"If we build less compute, whoever already has compute is holding something more valuable." The simulator agrees, mechanically. Block the pipeline and coverage drops, so rental prices rise. Hyperscaler capex and depreciation fall at the same time, which helps free cash flow. Run the "Clifton: revolt spreads" preset and Microsoft, Alphabet, Amazon and Meta come out flat against the base case. Suppliers fall 7–45%, and CoreWeave's equity is nearly wiped out. That's the same split the tape showed. It is also current market reality, not just theory: H100 rentals are ~$3.80–4.00/hr (up from ~$1.95 a year ago), B200 rentals are up 21.5% in 30 days, and providers are announcing Dec 1 hikes (GPU price tracker). Compute is getting scarcer, not cheaper.

His second-best point is that a local zoning revolt is now a macro event because data centers are a huge share of expected growth. The model can't capture that reflexivity, the way slower capex slows GDP, which hits the multiple. The "Market sentiment" slider is where you put it.

Accelerators Tight, easing

Nvidia Q2 FY27: $96.2B revenue, data center $89.0B (+117%). The Q3 guide is $108B ±2% with ~zero China, and it reports Nov 17. Vera Rubin is ~20% of Q3 data center revenue. Supply commitments jumped to $279B, mostly memory, and it says it is "supply constrained at least through FY28" (Nvidia).

AMD Q3: $13.4B (+45%), data center $8.5B (+97%), first MI455X/Helios revenue. Q4 guide ~$17B (AMD).

Broadcom AI revenue guide: $58B in FY26, ~$115B in FY27, ~$230B in FY28 (Fool). TPU output roughly doubled QoQ (Epoch).

CoWoS is ~130k wafers/month at end-2026 and ~185–200k at end-2027 (analyst estimates). TSMC is reportedly eyeing a +20% CoWoS price hike for 2027 (TrendForce). Packaging is easing as the bottleneck shifts to memory, optics and power.

Memory Sold out

Samsung: all 2027 HBM sold (Reuters). SK Hynix: DRAM and HBM "effectively sold out through 2029", demand >2× supply (PCMag). TrendForce: 2027 blended HBM ASP +121%, and 4Q26 contracts DRAM +10–15% and NAND +15–20% (TrendForce). Q3 DRAM revenue +369% YoY on only ~4% QoQ bit growth. This is all price (Counterpoint).

The wave: Hynix Yongin fab 1 ~Feb 2027, Micron Idaho ID1 first wafers mid-2027 and ID2 late 2028 (Idaho Statesman), Samsung P5 in 2028. CXMT goes ~300–350k → ~420k wpm by end-2027, though its HBM yields are only 20–30% (TrendForce).

The crack to watch: PC, phone and module inventories are elevated after H1 stockpiling, spot prices are weak, and PC OEMs are cutting next year's buys. The risk is a consumer air pocket, not an AI one.

Server CPUs Shortage

Intel's DCAI segment: $7.3B (+~80%). Lip-Bu Tan calls it "one of the most significant supply shortages in history", severe through 1H27, with Intel shifting capacity from client to server. Substrate capacity +40%, mostly in 2H27 (Tom's Hardware).

TrendForce: server CPU shipments +31% in 2026 and >40% in 2027, with Arm >20% share heading to ~30% (EMSNow). Arm: >60% of top-hyperscaler compute by end-2026 and DC royalties 2× (Arm).

v2's CPU thesis held on fundamentals. The stocks round-tripped anyway. See the Scorecard.

Optics Tightest after memory

Lumentum: AI optical parts sold out into early 2029, and it can't meet ~70% of demand for some products through 2027 (SemiMedia). Arista's Bechtolsheim says module lead times went from ~3 months to 9–12 months. Nvidia put $2B each into Lumentum and Coherent. New in v3: an optics capacity slider. In the Nov 3 consensus run, optics is what binds in 2028–30.

Power Binding

GE Vernova gas backlog plus reservations is 122 GW, with slots out to 2031. Siemens Energy lead times are 3+ years and large transformers ~128 weeks (secondary). PJM's 2028/29 auction cleared at the cap and came up 6.8 GW short, and FERC pushed the backstop auction to Feb–Mar 2027 (Utility Dive).

2027 US load forecasts run from 45 GW (EPRI peak) to 66 GW (Goldman demand) to 90 GW (Goldman capacity). The spread is about metric choice, so check which one anyone quotes (Goldman, EPRI). Near-term behind-the-meter power means gas. New SMRs don't produce commercial power before ~2030.

Cooling & electrical Demand strong, supply catching up

Vertiv Q3: sales $4.31B (+61%), organic orders >100% YoY, guide raised, ~$4.1B of customer prepayments (Grok summary; primary check pending). Customers prepaying is not what fading demand looks like. Vertiv is down 52% from its high and 23% since Nov 3. That's the single biggest gap in the model between fundamentals and price.

Usage

  • Google: >3.2 quadrillion tokens/month across surfaces (7× YoY). First-party API ~32B tokens/min, >4× YoY. Gemini app 1B+ MAU (Alphabet Q3).
  • ChatGPT: 1.2B weekly users (The Verge).

  • Big-4 2026 capex: ~$720–740B. MSFT trimmed to ~$170B on delivery timing. GOOGL is holding $195–205B and says 2027 will "increase significantly". What the balance sheets are saying

  • The buyers are borrowing. Free cash flow went negative at Alphabet (−$9.1B), Amazon (TTM −$42.4B), Meta (−$2.1B, first time in 10+ years) and Oracle. Only Microsoft is solidly positive. In one week Alphabet, Meta and Oracle raised more than $50B of bonds and equity. Amazon arranged up to an ~$80B secured supplier facility, and Meta created non-voting shares to fund AI (IFR, DCD).
  • The sellers are hoarding cash. Memory makers are throwing off cash at peak margins. In the model, Micron goes from ~$38B net cash to well over $200B by 2028 even in scenarios where its stock falls. Peak-cycle cash is why memory equities don't go to zero in a glut. They just go back to 6–8× normalized earnings.
  • The middle is levered. CoreWeave's CDS hit a record ~1,167 bp and Nvidia's 5-year CDS is up more than 3× on the year, with ~0.8 correlation to CoreWeave. Oracle's $18B New Mexico project loan traded ~89–91¢ and it plans another $20B raise (Reuters). These companies signed for chips and leases they must pay for whether or not a county lets them plug in, and the model's levered-builder logic reflects that. Slide the revolt up and their revenue falls faster than their capex.
  • Circularity: Nvidia has ~$30B into OpenAI, guarantees of up to ~$105B on an OpenAI Ohio lease, and a ~$6.3B capacity backstop at CoreWeave. A credit event in the middle layer travels both up and down the chain. That's what the "AI financing crunch" slider stands for.

Grading the last two reports against today, no cherry-picking. Prices are closes on the report date and Nov 6 intraday; Korea, Japan and Hong Kong use the Nov 5 close.

The short version: the fundamentals calls were right (memory sold out, CPU shortage, power binding) and the stock calls got run over. Every theme in both reports is still true on the supply side, yet the June buy list averages −35% and the October list about −25%. The lesson is the one the June report printed in bold and then ignored in its own ratings: this is a cyclical you rent, not a stock you marry. Exit discipline beat thesis quality by a mile. What I'd do differently, and did in v3

  • Price the politics. Neither earlier model had a node for "people who live next to the data center get a vote." v3 has seven political levers, each with probability and severity.
  • Calibrate to the market, then move off it. v1 and v2 gave absolute price targets. v3 anchors every stock to what the market actually paid on Nov 3 and shows only the change your assumptions imply. That's more honest about what a model like this can know.
  • Show the distribution. A Strong Buy on a beta-2.4 cyclical is a coin flip with a story attached. The Monte Carlo tab shows how wide the coin is.
  • The avoids worked, sort of. v2's three Avoids (CRWV, ORCL, OKLO) fell 16–43% since Oct 12. Then again, so did nearly everything else.

How the model works

Five years (2026–2030), five layers, one pipeline: demand → constrained supply → scarcity prices → company P&L → fair value. It's a thinking tool for orders of magnitude and directions, not a DCF you should trade off.

1. Demand (M H100-equivalents of compute needed)

2026 starts at 36M H100e against a 33M installed fleet (~92% coverage), consistent with rising rental prices. The split is training 38%, consumer 26%, enterprise/agents 33% and robotics 3%. Each segment compounds on its own sliders: users × usage, adoption × agent intensity, robots in service, training growth. Inference segments are then multiplied by a capability unlock term, (1 + unlock)doublings, and an efficiency term, (1 + efficiency)t·(ε−1), where ε is the Jevons elasticity. At ε = 1, efficiency is a wash. Below 1 it shrinks hardware demand, and above 1 cheaper tokens pull in more than they save.

2. Supply (what gets added each year)

Additions = the minimum of five caps. Buyers' demand is the gap to demand, reduced by policy drag (expensing, power tariffs, Section 232, the financing crunch), with a floor of 65% of the prior year for contracts and momentum. Chips/CoWoS start at 14M H100e in 2026. HBM bit growth is adjusted for HBM per unit of compute falling ~8%/yr. Optics is the fourth cap. Power is the GW that actually energize. That starts at ~11 GW in 2026, ~65% of announcements landing on time, and is reduced by the US share × blocked share, plus relocated capacity a year later. New compute's kW per H100e falls with perf-per-watt. Defaults are set so chips, power and optics co-bind around 2027–28, which matches what the industry is reporting.

Politics: the revolt's expected value (probability × severity) applies at (1 − locked share) in 2027 and in full in 2028, then trends by the backlash slider. In the Simulator, each risk enters at its expected value. The "all happen" and "none happen" switches override probabilities (Taiwan excepted). In Monte Carlo, each risk is a real coin flip.

3. Prices

  • Compute rental = (coverage ÷ 2026 coverage)−0.8, clamped 0.5–1.8.
  • Chip pricing power falls when chips are not the bottleneck (utilization0.8) and rises with overall scarcity. This is how a power-bound world shifts value from Nvidia to compute owners.
  • DRAM / NAND = (demand ÷ supply, relative to 2026)3 × a 1.35/1.30 carry-over for exit-2026 contract hikes. DRAM demand is conventional servers and devices (tied to CPU volume) plus HBM wafers (tied to accelerator additions). Supply grows at the bit-supply slider, plus ~20%/yr extra once the capacity wave lands.
  • CPU ∝ (supply ÷ demand)−1, where demand = general-purpose sockets + accelerators × CPU ratio.
  • Power gets a scarcity premium when utilization runs above 90%, minus the price-cap politics.

4. Companies

Each company is built from 2026 revenue (calendar-year estimate from trailing data and run-rates), a normalized operating margin, its AI share, and a mix of "pools" (GPU, ASIC, networking, CPU, foundry, DRAM, NAND, fab equipment, servers, power gear, power generation, memory interface). Each pool has a volume index, a price index and a unit-cost index, so price changes fall straight to margin while volume changes scale revenue and cost together. China exposure is cut by the export slider. Compute owners (hyperscalers, Oracle, CoreWeave) work differently. Their AI revenue = fleet × rental price, and costs include depreciation of a 5-year capex stack. Oracle and CoreWeave are levered: their capex is mostly committed, but revenue needs energized sites.

5. Valuation and calibration

Equity value = (2028 NOPAT, or EBITDA for compute owners) × multiple − 2028 net debt, discounted 2 years. Net debt evolves with the scenario's free cash flow, so balance sheets matter. The multiple is calibrated per company so that the Nov 3 consensus preset reproduces the Nov 3 close exactly. It then flexes with 2028→30 growth relative to that calibration, the discount rate and the sentiment slider. A Taiwan haircut applies to fab-exposed names in proportion to the annual probability. Fair value is therefore always "what the market paid before the election, adjusted for how your world differs from the one it priced."

What it can't see

  • Positioning, momentum, rates and the Samsung guide. Some of every drop isn't politics. That's why "implied % blocked" over 70% means "something else did this."
  • Reflexivity: slower capex → slower GDP → lower multiples everywhere. Use the sentiment slider.
  • Absolute earnings levels. Company paths are index-driven and won't match consensus EPS. The calibration absorbs the level, so read the changes.
  • Geography beyond US/non-US. A single substation can bind a whole campus.
