Tap Notes: Who Holds the Key

November 20, 2026

Three items this time, all about the same question: who holds the key, and who gets to say something is true? One tool decided that for you and never mentioned it. One keeps everything on your disk. One put a formal verifier between the model and the truth. Smaller digest, tighter thread.

Inside ZCode: Silently uploading your Git history to the cloud (September 2026)

A reverse-engineering writeup found that ZCode, a desktop AI coding app, packaged entire workspaces, full .git history included. It encrypted them with a server-supplied RSA public key and uploaded them to cloud storage. No UI setting turned it off, and the privacy policy didn’t mention it. The author’s workaround was to make the local checkpoints directory immutable at the filesystem level. A companion analysis puts the .git directory at roughly 86% of the payload.

Why it matters: The upload ran in a host-level sidecar, not through the agent’s tool list. Auditing the tools and toggling permissions would never have caught it. If you vet coding agents by reading their tool surface, you’re inspecting the front door while the loading dock is open.

The .git detail is the one to take to your team. Git history holds deleted secrets, rotated keys, unpushed branch names and internal hostnames. A snapshot of the working tree is bad. A snapshot of the history is everything you thought you’d removed.

The encryption design says the rest. When only the vendor holds the private key, that isn’t a backup for you. It’s collection that happens to be encrypted.

Server-held RSA key means the encryption is designed for the vendor, not for you.

Post to X

The defense is also the useful part. You can’t fight an uploader you can’t configure, but you can starve it by making the write target unwritable. That’s worth remembering for any sandbox you build around a closed harness.

I vibed a proof of Conway’s conjecture

A self-described math novice spent a month steering LLM agents and the Lean proof assistant toward a formal proof of a 50-year-old conjecture. The setup used multi-agent “laboratories” with roles: PM, math, red team, Lean. It also involved repeated burn-it-down restarts. The proof has passed mechanical checks and is still waiting on a human mathematician.

Why it matters: This is the most honest agentic-coding methodology post in the pile, wearing a math costume. The failure mode to steal is the “theorem tower.” The models invented their own vocabulary, then cited it as load-bearing. Anyone fine-tuning or chaining agents has seen the code version of this: a made-up abstraction that every later step treats as real.

The fixes port straight to software:

  • Ground truth you can’t argue with. Lean played that role. In code, it’s a conformance test suite.
  • Speculation stays slightly ahead of formalization, never far ahead. The checker has to be close behind.
  • Separate worktrees for stable and experimental. Restarts stay cheap that way.

The number to remember is the price. The author says it cost around $40k and could have been 5-10x cheaper with better steering. Verification discipline is a cost lever as well as a quality one.

Hister: A private search engine for the pages you visit and the files you keep

Hister is an AGPL-licensed, Go-based personal search engine. It indexes the pages you browse and the files you keep, and you query it from a web UI, a terminal, or an MCP-connected assistant. It runs locally with no telemetry by default. Semantic search is optional and uses an embeddings endpoint you configure.

Why it matters: After ZCode, the contrast is the point. This is the same shape of tool (an agent-adjacent index of everything you touch) with the key staying in your hands. The MCP endpoint makes it a retriever your assistant can call for “what have I actually read about this,” without that history leaving your machine.

Bring-your-own embeddings is the right default. The index is useful without a model, and the model is a choice you make rather than a dependency you inherit. I’m putting it on the basement box and pointing an agent at it.

The three links make one argument. Trust the thing that runs where you can see it, and make claims prove themselves against something that can’t be flattered. 🪨
