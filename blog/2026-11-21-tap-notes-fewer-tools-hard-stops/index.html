Tap Notes: Fewer Tools, Hard Stops

November 21, 2026

Most of what I read this week was about subtraction. One harness cut its tools from 21 to 5. One loop got a ceiling on how many times it may try again. One claimed proof is only interesting because a machine can check it. The model matters less than the constraints around it.

Harness design

chad: why the tool surface is five tools

The design doc for a local coding agent that runs a 27B model on a laptop. It owns the inference process and keeps a persistent KV cache. Version 1.x had 21 tools. Version 2.x has five, after trajectory data showed the extras weren’t earning their prompt tokens.

Why it matters: The best line is about the shell. The model has seen a billion shell sessions in pretraining, so it reaches for the shell instead of your custom tool dialect. Every bespoke tool you add is a bet against that prior. Also worth stealing: help goes inside tool results, not into more tools. Teach the model at the moment it fails instead of paying for the lesson on every request.

The model reaches for the shell it saw a billion times in pretraining.
Post to X

I Came, I Prompted, I Left, Part 1: Building a Custom Hypervisor for the MacBook Neo

Cody Ho spent two months, mostly on unattended Codex loops, building a hypervisor that boots macOS on Apple silicon the existing tooling doesn’t support. The post covers the debugging milestones (interrupt storms, a timer the agent hallucinated at 41.66667x speed) and what he learned about working with agents.

Why it matters: It’s an honest account of long unattended runs. The process lessons are the product: understand the problem before the agent starts, force periodic simplification, and don’t let it quietly disable features to make a test pass. The experiment log pattern is the one to copy. It gives the agent somewhere to put what it learned before context compaction erases it.

Knowing when to stop

Six Codex Rounds In, the Babysit Loop Called Time

An automated review-and-fix loop hit its six-round ceiling on a PR. It stopped and handed the decision to a human instead of writing a seventh patch. The author’s diagnosis is that the regex approach never converged because it guessed at intent when a structured signal (the list of operations each server actually exposes) was available.

Why it matters: The regex isn’t the lesson. The ceiling is. When every fix spawns a narrower fix, the loop is telling you the approach is wrong, and a hard limit is the only thing that makes anyone listen. Pair it with a mandatory “here’s what’s unresolved” writeup at the stop. Every loop that fixes things on its own should have both.

Machines checking machines

Scientific American: Anthropic mathematician claims Riemann proof

Levent Alpöge, a mathematician at Anthropic, says he has proven the Riemann hypothesis and its generalized form with Claude’s help. The proof is written in Lean. Verification is still pending, and mathematicians quoted in the article call the result potentially transformative.

Why it matters: Wait for the checker before believing anything. The interesting part isn’t the headline, it’s the structure. An agent produces an artifact and an independent mechanical verifier accepts or rejects it, so the trust comes from the verifier and not from anyone’s confidence. I want to see the Lean proof pass the whole verification cycle first. If it does, that’s the strongest case yet for this pattern.

The agent produces an artifact, an independent machine checks it. Verification isn’t vibes.
Post to X

Language Models Can Rewrite Their Own Weights

A new paper reports a 27B model rewriting its own weights through reasoning alone, with no gradients and no training run, and improving across sessions.

Why it matters: If it holds up, it’s a new axis for self-improving agents, one that skips the training pipeline. I haven’t gone through the evals yet. Read it with one question in mind: what independently checks that the rewritten weights are better and not just different? Without a verifier, self-modification is just drift.

The OS is an agent now

Signal Is Serious About Protecting Your Privacy

Signal pushes back on OS-level AI agents (Gemini, Siri, Recall) reading app content, including its use of screenshot blocking to keep them out.

Why it matters: The lethal trifecta now operates at the operating-system level. The assistant sees your private data and ingests untrusted content, and it can act. An app that wants to keep its contents away from that assistant has to ask the platform for help, because the app can’t enforce it alone. Expect more of these fights.

Take away the extra tools, the unlimited retries and the unverified claims, and what’s left is the agent doing the work. That’s the idea this week. 🪨
