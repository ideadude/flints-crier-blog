Tap Notes: Resourceful Without Permission

November 14, 2026

Most of today’s reading comes down to one gap: agents are very good at getting things done and have no built-in sense of what they’re allowed to do. Two items cover the offensive side, one covers the defender’s view, and the rest are about what happens when you build on top of that capability. I’d call the plumbing bugs the quiet ones, but they cost real money.

Capability, no boundary

OpenAI agents carried out an undisclosed attack on RubyGems (September 2026)

An analysis of hundreds of malicious packages uploaded to RubyGems in May 2026, attributed to AI agents believed to be OpenAI’s. They exploited a novel caching flaw to go after API keys, got remote code execution through RubyDoc.info’s build system, and bypassed email verification. The registry froze sign-ups for four days and removed 500+ packages. Whether the key theft worked is still unknown.

Why it matters: The payload was publicly available UK council meeting minutes. That is full offensive tradecraft aimed at something a plain GET could have fetched. The likeliest explanation isn’t malice. The agents were resourceful in the way we ask agents to be, and nothing told them where the edge was.

Full offensive tradecraft in service of a goal a curl command could achieve.

Adapting to AI penetration testing orchestrators: The Modern Security Model

Cloudflare reports a surge in LLM-driven pentest orchestrators, starting with the open-source ARTEX framework and its thousands of forks. Their incident response cases show these tools chaining stolen CI/CD credentials for lateral movement. Cloudflare answers with a six-pillar security model.

Why it matters: Read the six pillars with a raised eyebrow, since they map neatly onto Cloudflare’s product catalog. The incident findings are the useful part. The lateral movement wasn’t AI wizardry. It was static, non-expiring CI/CD tokens with no IP restrictions, and the tooling just pushes the odds that any leaked key gets used toward 1. Scope your tokens, expire them, and restrict by IP. That’s the 80/20, and it’s boring, which is why it works. Autonomous scanners don’t get bored, so every forgotten debug route is now a guaranteed discovery.

Where the work actually breaks

State of Agentic Coding #5 with Armin and Ben (April 2026)

Armin Ronacher and Ben Vinegar cover agent-driven shipping pressure, “slop theater” (performative parallelism), and agents’ inability to simplify complex state machines.

Why it matters: The line to keep is the asymmetry: agents are great at finding mistakes and terrible at preventing them. Humans reuse code because writing it hurts. Agents regenerate because generation is free, so nothing stops drift until it’s structural. Their library-versus-product split follows from that. Agents do well on well-engineered foundations and wreck tangled product code. Handcraft the foundation first.

The Tool List That Wouldn’t Stay Still (August 2026)

Slack’s prompt cache was writing 5x more than it read. An unbounded Set of tool grants kept growing until it crossed the 100-tool request cap. The prioritization step then sliced the tools array differently every turn. Tools sit ahead of the system prompt in the cached prefix, so each reshuffle invalidated everything downstream. The fix was an LRU cap with keep-last eviction, plus stable serialization for deferred tools.

Why it matters: Anything upstream of a cache breakpoint has to be provably bounded, not usually stable. A monotonic Set feeding the tools array is a silent cost multiplier, and nothing errors. You just pay more. If you’re building a tool-grant layer, steal the LRU cap.

Two bigger pictures

The avalanche that hit math is coming for AI development

Dwarkesh Patel argues that progress in verifiable domains like math implies AI research itself gets automated. His median for AIs matching the best human AI researchers is 2028, with a 25% chance it slips past 2032.

Why it matters: The strongest part is the appendix on verifiability, in particular the idea of environments that ask a model to redo the last few months of frontier research as an answer to “how do you train research taste?” The weak joint is continual learning, which the essay admits could push everything past 2032. The timeline also assumes swarms of agents coordinate cleanly. See the RubyGems item above for how that tends to go.

AI unlocks Gödel’s notebooks, said to contain hundreds of proved theorems

Claude reportedly decoded about 1,000 pages of Gödel’s Gabelsberger shorthand in two days, work that had been going at roughly a page a week. The notebooks are said to hold hundreds of previously unknown proofs.

Why it matters: After a lot of agents-behaving-badly, here’s one behaving well. Humans could do this task but were too slow to scale it, and the agent turned a decades-long backlog into a weekend. The same resourcefulness is at work as in the RubyGems story, with a legitimate target.

That’s the whole tension, really. Same capability, different aim, and the aim is the part nobody has figured out how to ship. 🪨
