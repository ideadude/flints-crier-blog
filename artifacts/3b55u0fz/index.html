Section II — The AssetTwenty years. Two founders. Zero investors.

In 2006, Jason and Kim Coleman started Stranger Studios as a two-person web consultancy. In 2011 they released Paid Memberships Pro, an open source membership plugin for WordPress, and spent four years nights-and-weekends-ing it until the revenue let them go full time in 2015. No seed round. No accelerator. No pivot story. Just twenty years of compounding.

Today Paid Memberships Pro powers 90,000+ membership sites — yoga studios, newspapers, associations, course creators, communities — with a team of about 14 people. The product is open source. The business is wildly profitable anyway. That combination confuses investors, which we enjoy.

Section IV — Growth ProspectsWhere the next $10M of revenue hides

Licensing is the trunk. The branches are bigger than the trunk. Ninety thousand sites already trust us with the most sensitive part of their business — billing — and roughly 93% of them pay us nothing today. That is not a weakness. That is the single largest unmonetized membership-site audience on the open web, sitting one checkbox away from paid services.

1. Managed hosting — launching now

We host and manage the whole stack — site, plugin, payments, updates — at roughly $100/month per customer with ~50% gross margin. The internal goal ramp: 570 customers by end of 2026, 2,010 by end of 2027. That’s a $2.4M annualized run rate by late 2027, layered on top of everything above. Membership sites are the stickiest hosting customers in existence: their revenue flows through the install. Nobody migrates the machine that pays them.

2. Services on the install base — modeled

Things every membership site needs and currently buys from strangers: spam & security, tax handling, video hosting, transactional email, file storage. Our internal model, at conservative attach rates against just the existing paid base, pencils to roughly $960K/year in incremental revenue (~$845K gross profit). The distribution cost of these products is a settings page we already own.

3. Payments rev-share — live and expanding

The Stripe platform business already produces ~$900K/year and grows with member volume, not with our headcount. Billing optimization, tax, chargeback protection, and BNPL rev-shares model to another ~$200K/year on current volume — more as hosting concentrates payments onto our platform account.

4. Beyond membership sites — the big swing

“Managed WordPress with billing built in” is not a membership-site product. It’s a product for every course creator, association, newsletter, community, and small SaaS that invoices anyone. The same stack hosts all of them. The hosting TAM is not 90,000 sites; it’s every business on the open web that charges money and doesn’t want a platform taking 10%.

Section V — Untapped LeversSix knobs we deliberately haven’t turned

We optimize for a calm, profitable, hundred-year company. A buyer with different incentives would find the cupboard full:

Free → paid conversion

93% of 90,000 sites pay $0. Each point of conversion at current pricing is roughly $300K/year. We convert gently. You probably wouldn’t.

Pricing power

We price like the bootstrapped Pennsylvanians we are. Competitors charge 2–3× for less product and worse support, and their customers stay anyway.

Hosting attach

Every support ticket, onboarding flow, and checkout is a hosting lead we don’t currently work. The funnel exists; we just haven’t aimed it.

Payments concentration

Move more of the install base onto the payments platform and the rev-share compounds on member volume — revenue that grows while everyone sleeps.

Enterprise & agencies

Associations and publishers run six-figure membership operations on us today, self-serve, on the same plans as a yoga teacher. There is no enterprise tier. There could be by Thursday.

The boring superpower

40% margins fund all of the above with zero outside capital. This machine builds its own expansions. It just needs more ambition than two comfortable founders currently supply.

    Section VIII — Anticipated ObjectionsFAQ

    Is this a joke?
    The tone is playful. The financials are real. The price is the price. We’ve found that one page of honesty filters better than four calls of mutual evaluation.
    Why publish your revenue?
    Because every associate asks for it on call one anyway, under an NDA that protects nobody from anything. Also: the numbers are good, and we know it.
    Would you actually sell?
    At $50M, we would have a serious conversation. That is precisely what the number means — it’s the threshold where “no thanks” becomes “go on.”
    Isn’t $50 million a high multiple for this business?
    Tremendously. At 2026 numbers it’s roughly 19× revenue and 49× profit — Kajabi raised at 20× revenue, Substack at ~24×, and we are aware we are neither. But that math prices a company whose owners have to sell. We don’t. This is a control premium on a profitable, growing, founder-run machine that gets more valuable every year we keep it — so the number sits exactly where losing it would stop hurting. If your model says we’re worth less, run Section VI with your own assumptions and enjoy the good news: you don’t have to buy it.
    Why is the slider capped at $200M?
    Above $200M the website starts negotiating against itself, and our lawyer (we have one, singular) asked us to stop.
    What happens to my information?
    It goes into a database, where it is studied, scored, and — let us be perfectly clear — sold, if anyone offers to buy it. We are a transparency company. The projected proceeds from selling your data are already included in the $50,000,000 valuation. See Section VII.
